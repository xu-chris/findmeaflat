# Demand Data as a Funding Model

Your open question — *does the government already get this data from the platforms?* —
has a clear answer, and it splits cleanly in two.

**Supply data: yes, they already buy it, and the market is consolidated.**
**Demand data: no, and nobody observes it.**

That distinction decides everything about whether this business model works.

---

## 1. What the German state already has

### It buys listing data commercially

The BBSR's *Wohnungsmarktbeobachtung* is built on purchased portal data:

- **IDN ImmoDaten GmbH** (2004–2011), then **empirica-systeme**
- **ImmoScout24 listings** specifically, for analysing *Angebotsmieten* in the
  14 largest cities

**empirica-systeme has since been integrated into VALUE AG** (now "VALUE Marktdaten"),
and both have belonged to the **Hypoport Group** since 2018/2019. Their database covers
rental flats, condominiums, houses, plots, student housing, micro-apartments and
furnished units, and they explicitly sell into *kommunale Wohnungsmarktbeobachtung* —
the exact municipal customer this model targets.

So the supply side is not a gap. It is an established market with a consolidated,
listed-company incumbent holding licensed access.

### Its demand forecasting is demographic modelling, not observation

The BBSR *Wohnungsbedarfsprognose* projects roughly **320,000 units per year to 2030**,
with a demographic component of about 160,000/year. Its central data basis is the
**Zensus 2022**.

That is a top-down population model. It answers *how many households will exist and
where*. It does not and cannot answer *what those households are actually looking for,
at what budget, in which streets, and what they fail to find.*

### The state says its own data is inadequate

The BBSR's own assessment, and this is the opening this model would aim at: no single
rent-data source currently satisfies the requirements of being representative, current,
time-series capable and regionally differentiated all at once, and there is an urgent
need to expand data on existing rents, offered rents and newly agreed rents. There is a
live 2025 departmental research programme — *"Verbesserung der wohnungspolitisch
relevanten Grundlagen zu Mietdaten"* — funded to address exactly this.

**Conclusion: the demand-side gap is real and officially acknowledged.** Your instinct
was right. The rest of this document is about whether *this* project can fill it.

## 2. The thesis, stated precisely

Every `Search` row is a declared, revealed preference: this city, these districts, this
size, this budget, at this moment. Aggregated across users and time, that yields
something no census, no Mietspiegel and no listing database contains:

| Measure | Who has it today |
|---|---|
| What is offered, where, at what price | VALUE AG, F+B, bulwiengesa, GEWOS — sold to BBSR and municipalities |
| How many households will exist, where | Destatis / Zensus 2022 → BBSR projections |
| **What people are searching for, at what budget, where** | **nobody** |
| **Which searches return nothing — unmet demand** | **nobody** |
| **How far real budgets sit below asking rents** | **nobody** |

The last three are the product. "In Wedding, 340 active searches for 2-room flats under
900 € warm produced 6 matching listings in 30 days" is a sentence no existing dataset
can produce, and it is precisely the sentence a housing planner or a developer's
site-selection team wants.

---

## 3. The three hard problems

Being straight about these, because each one can sink the model on its own.

### 3.1 Provenance — the one that matters most

**Listing data obtained by scraping against portal terms of service is not sellable to a
public authority.** Public bodies run procurement due diligence; "we crawled it without
permission" fails that check, and it fails it at exactly the moment a contract would
otherwise be signed. VALUE AG's position rests on licensed access, which is the asset
this project does not have and cannot cheaply acquire.

**But the demand data has completely different provenance.** It is generated by your own
users, in your own system, with their knowledge and (if designed correctly) their
consent. It is not scraped from anyone. That is a clean chain of title.

**Therefore: never try to sell supply data. Sell — or publish — only demand data.**
This is the single most important line in this document. It also happens to be the
non-obvious, uncontested half.

### 3.2 Scale and sampling bias

Statistical validity needs volume, and a demand dataset built from a few hundred
Telegram users in Berlin is not a dataset anyone will pay for.

Worse, the sample is self-selected in a way a statistician at BBSR will identify
immediately: users of a German-language Telegram flat-hunting bot skew young, digitally
literate, urban, and are already the most motivated searchers in the market. That bias
is not fatal — every survey has one — but it must be **measured and disclosed**, not
discovered by the buyer.

Realistic threshold: **low thousands of active searchers in a single city** before the
signal is defensible for that city. That is a growth problem before it is a data
problem, and it means the funding model cannot bootstrap the product; the product has to
work first, for its own reasons.

### 3.3 Selling to the state is slow and indirect

Public procurement (Vergaberecht) and annual budget cycles make direct sales to BBSR
unrealistic for a small operator. The BBSR commissions *Ressortforschung* through
tendered research contracts, typically awarded to established institutes.

The realistic routes are therefore:

1. **Research partner / subcontractor** to an institute already holding BBSR contracts
   (empirica, IW Köln, GEWOS, Pestel). Slow, low-margin, but a real door — and the
   novelty of the data is the reason they would take the call.
2. **Municipal customers directly.** A single *Stadt* buying a demand report for its own
   housing planning is a far shorter sale than a federal one, and municipalities already
   buy *Wohnungsmarktbeobachtung* products.
3. **Private buyers** — developers, *Projektentwickler*, *Wohnungsbaugenossenschaften*.
   Faster decisions, real budgets for *Standortanalyse*. They already buy from VALUE AG,
   bulwiengesa and F+B, so the pitch has to be the demand angle specifically, not a
   cheaper version of what they have.
4. **Grant funding rather than sales.** See §5.

## 4. GDPR — workable, with one trap designed in from the start

The legal position is more comfortable than it first appears, but only if built in.

**Truly anonymised data falls outside the GDPR entirely** — no consent required, because
it is no longer personal data. Processing personal data *for statistical purposes* is
covered by **Art. 89 GDPR** and **§27 BDSG**, whose core condition is that the *results*
must be aggregated data, not personal data, and must never be used for measures or
decisions concerning individual natural persons.

That maps onto this product exactly: the output is "340 searches for 2-room flats under
900 € in Wedding", never "Chris searched for X".

**The trap is small-cell re-identification.** In a thinly-populated area with few users,
"3 searches for 4-room flats under 1.200 € in Alt-Treptow in the last week" can identify
a person, especially combined with anything else. Requirements, non-negotiable:

- **k-anonymity thresholds** — suppress any cell below a minimum count (k ≥ 20 is a
  defensible starting point for published figures, higher for fine geography)
- **Geographic floor** — aggregate to district or LOR level, never street or address
- **Temporal floor** — monthly or quarterly windows, never live
- **No cross-tabulation that reconstructs an individual** from multiple suppressed cells
- **Never join demand data back to a `telegram_chat_id`** in anything leaving the system

**On the legal basis, take the stronger option.** Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interest with
Art. 89 safeguards would probably suffice for internal statistics. But if the output is
ever sold, **explicit opt-in at `/start` is both safer and better product**:

> 📊 Wir werten anonym aus, **wann in welcher Gegend nach welcher Art von Wohnung
> gesucht wird** — nie, *wer* sucht. Diese Statistiken helfen Städten und Planern zu
> sehen, wo Wohnraum fehlt, und finanzieren diesen Bot.
> [✅ Einverstanden]  [❌ Nein danke — der Bot funktioniert trotzdem]

Three reasons this framing is right beyond compliance: it is your own stated principle
("not who — when, what, where"); a refusal must genuinely not degrade the service, or
the consent is not freely given and is legally worthless; and it is an attractive pitch
to exactly the people who use this. "Funded by selling anonymous shortage statistics to
public planners" is a far better answer to *how is this free?* than any advertising
model, to an audience that is structurally sympathetic to the housing-shortage cause.

## 5. The stronger alternative: publish it

Worth putting on the table, because it may beat selling.

An **open Wohnungsnachfrage-Index** — published monthly, free, per city and district —
would:

- Establish the dataset as *the* reference before any incumbent notices the gap
- Generate press in a country where the housing shortage is a permanent front-page topic,
  which is also the cheapest user acquisition available
- Make the data more valuable, not less, by making it citable
- Change the funding conversation from **procurement** (slow, tendered, hostile to small
  operators) to **Förderung / Zuwendung** (grants for a public-interest data project) —
  a route where a small independent operator is a *plausible* recipient rather than a
  structurally disadvantaged bidder
- Sidestep the provenance problem entirely, since nothing is being sold

Revenue then comes from bespoke analysis on top of the free index — the standard open-core
shape — rather than from access to the base data.

This is a genuine fork in the road, not a detail. **It does not need deciding now**, but
it should be decided before the first buyer conversation, because the two paths diverge
hard on exclusivity.

## 6. What Phase 1 must do to keep this possible

Small, cheap, and each one is expensive to retrofit:

1. **Timestamp everything on `Search` and `Filter`.** `inserted_at`, `updated_at`,
   `activated_at`, `paused_at`, `deleted_at`. The demand signal is inherently temporal;
   a search with no history is worth far less.
2. **Never hard-delete a `Search`.** Soft-delete (`ash_archival`), so a withdrawn search
   still contributes to the historical series. A user deleting their *account* is a
   different matter and must genuinely erase.
3. **Record the outcome, not just the query.** How many listings matched each search per
   period. Unmet demand — searches returning nothing — is the most valuable single
   number in this dataset and is only computable if match counts are stored per window.
4. **Store the consent decision as data**, with a timestamp and the wording version
   consented to. "We asked in German at some point" is not a defence; a versioned record
   is.
5. **Geography at district/LOR granularity from the start**, not free-text city names.
   Aggregation needs a stable spatial key, and normalising a year of free text later is
   the same avoidable pain as retro-geocoding.
6. **Keep demand aggregates in their own resource**, computed by a scheduled job, never
   queried live from `Search` rows. That boundary is what makes k-anonymity enforceable
   in one place instead of at every call site.

None of this requires deciding whether to sell, publish or do neither. It only keeps all
three open.

---

## 7. Honest assessment

**The gap is real** — confirmed, and officially acknowledged by the BBSR's own research
programme. Nobody observes revealed housing demand in Germany.

**The provenance split is what makes it viable** — the supply data is unsellable and the
market is taken; the demand data is clean, uncontested and yours.

**The blocker is scale, not law or technology.** This needs thousands of active searchers
in a city before anyone pays for it, which means the funding model cannot come first.
**Build the product because it is useful; the dataset accrues as a side effect.** If it
reaches scale, this becomes a genuine option — and Phase 1's six small design choices
above are what keep the door open at almost no cost.

Treat it as a **strategic reason to store data carefully today**, not as a revenue plan
to build toward.
